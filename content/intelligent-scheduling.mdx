<Callout label="TL;DR">

An AI scheduling engine for hospitality that learns how each restaurant runs (forecasts, roles, preferences, compliance) and drafts the week for the general manager to review. Designing for **trust over pure automation**, with a review step and visible reasoning, won over sceptical managers. The beta signed Nory's **largest enterprise customer**, with **£5M+** in projected labour savings for 2025, and became a key proof point ahead of the **Series B**.

## 01. Problem

Labour can account for up to 40% of revenue in hospitality, and the weekly schedule is one of the biggest levers a restaurant has to control it.

Nory already had the forecasting data needed to build better schedules than a GM could manually. We could predict demand, understand staffing requirements, and calculate labour cost. What we had not done was put any of it where the scheduling decision actually happens.

## 02. What we discovered

I led discovery across interviews, workshops, usage data and workflow analysis. Without forecasted demand in view, managers **overstaffed quiet periods and understaffed busy ones**, raising labour cost and hurting service at the same time.

Building one week meant juggling forecasts, labour budgets, availability, roles, compliance rules and department needs, mostly in spreadsheets outside the product. Usage data showed it: **78%** of schedules were still built by hand, cell by cell.

The numbers were only half of it. GMs distrusted algorithmic recommendations on principle: years of software had taught them that automation means losing control.

<Quote by="General manager, interview">
  "Well... the AI doesn't know that the main door was broken yesterday, does it?"
</Quote>

So we optimised for **trust first**, and let accuracy compound behind it.

## 03. Exploring options

With the requirements mapped, I explored where AI could add value across the scheduling workflow: inline recommendations, fully generated schedules, conversational concepts.

The question underneath all of them was the same. Where does the AI decide, and where does the manager stay in control?

## 04. The solution

We brought forecasted demand directly into the scheduling workflow.

The weekly view surfaces sales, orders, labour cost and staffing by department. The daily view goes an hour at a time, showing current against optimal staffing, with a recommendation inline when the week drifts off budget.

<MediaContainer

We also made the AI's reasoning visible. Every recommendation could be traced back to the forecast, historical orders per labour hour (OPLH) and labour targets, so a GM had enough context to understand why a given shift was suggested, and enough standing to disagree with it.

"Create schedule" opens a short sequence that narrates each step, from analysing the forecast to generating the week day by day, with the data sources named in plain language.

## 05. What we scoped out

We cut custom rule editing, conversational AI, department colour coding, and a refresh of legacy design system components. We had also planned a second intelligence layer that turns natural language into business rules. Instead we focused on proving the core bet: that AI scheduling saves money by optimising labour hours.

## 06. Impact

The £5M+ figure came from scaling the beta to that customer's full estate: roughly £5.3M a year against £68.1M of labour spend.

## 07. What I learned

When AI operates inside a workflow owned by an expert, trust matters more than autonomy.

The AI does not need to prove it is always right. It needs to make its work understandable, editable and reversible.

## 08. Questions that come up

<Faq>
  <FaqItem question="What was your role?">
    Senior Product Designer, and the only person on the product side for the duration of the project. Every product decision (which constraints to weigh, which variables to surface, what the AI's outputs look like in the canvas) landed with me.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Who was on the team?">
    A machine learning engineer I partnered with on the model, plus three engineers and a tech lead. There was no PM during the build; a Head of Product joined toward the end.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="What AI model did you use?">
    We built our own: a linear regression model trained on the customer's historical orders and staffing allocation, tuned on the operational signals we wanted it to weigh. Not a general-purpose LLM bolted onto the workflow. I worked closely with the ML engineer on its design.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="How does the algorithm work?">
    Two parts in sequence. Staffing prediction works out how many people each department needs, hour by hour, from forecasted demand. Allocation decides which employee gets each shift. My job was surfacing both (headcount, timing, department and the named employee) so a GM could read a proposal at a glance and override any part of it without leaving the canvas.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="What constraints feed the model?">
    Five layers: availability (holidays, sickness, time off), compliance rules (minimum rest between shifts), contracted hours per week, cost of labour, and the GM's own habits. If someone always closes on Friday, the model leans toward keeping them there.
  </FaqItem>
</Faq>
